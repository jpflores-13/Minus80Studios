## Evidence table: the escalation level (playable octopus, chased by a blue shark)

After the three-snail campaign, a short escalation level flips the roles: the player controls an octopus hunting
cone snails scattered across the reef (cone snails are confirmed solitary foragers, not schooling prey — see the
new row below), while a blue shark (_Prionace glauca_) threatens the octopus the way the octopus threatens the
snail in the main campaign. The character is _Octopus bimaculoides_ (California two-spot octopus). The blue shark
is named on screen because it is one of the species Galván-Magaña 2013 documents preying on cephalopods (below);
its detection ability borrows a mechanism (electroreception) demonstrated in a different, related species (Kalmijn
1971), not a blue-shark-specific finding — see the simplification note on that row, and "Blue shark chosen over a
hammerhead or a whale shark" below the table.

**Cipher's core mechanic was reworked** from a shared aim/lock/reel skeleton (identical to Catalyst's) to two
genuinely different systems — Camouflage and a synchronous-grip Pounce — so the octopus's toolkit no longer feels
like a relabeled cone snail. See "Gameplay model: the escalation level" below for how each is implemented, and
"Open questions" for the generalisations each one leans on.

**Blue shark chosen over a hammerhead or a whale shark:** Galván-Magaña 2013 documents six shark species preying on
cephalopods — silky shark, blue shark, scalloped hammerhead, smooth hammerhead, pelagic thresher, and bigeye
thresher. The blue shark was picked as the most immediately recognisable "shark" silhouette for the game's small
pixel-art sprite; a hammerhead would also be defensible on the same citation. A whale shark was considered and
rejected: whale sharks are filter feeders (plankton, krill, small fish) and are not documented predators of
octopuses or other cephalopods, so using one as Cipher's threat would not be supportable. Sharks generally hunt
alone; true coordinated pack-hunting is not well documented for the species above. Some sharks aggregate or school
for non-hunting reasons (for example, scalloped hammerheads are well known to school by day), but that is a
different, species-specific behaviour from blue sharks, and is not claimed here.

## Evidence table: the second escalation level (playable blue shark, hunted by an orca pod)

A second escalation level flips the roles again: the blue shark that threatened Cipher is now playable (Glaucus,
_Prionace glauca_ — the same species, same art, not a duplicate), hunting squid scattered in open water, while a
**pod of orcas** (_Orcinus orca_) hunts Glaucus the way Glaucus hunted cone snails, except as a coordinating group
instead of one animal. Real orca pack-hunting of sharks is well documented, but **not against the blue shark**: the
two sources below document it against juvenile whale sharks and a white shark, not _Prionace glauca_. The game
honestly reuses that real, coordinated behavior for a different shark species — see "Orca pod chosen over a direct

**Glaucus's core mechanic was reworked** from the shared aim/lock/reel skeleton to a chase: Pursuit (lock onto the
nearest squid and swim hard at it, no cursor aim) and Burst (buy more time mid-chase), replacing a single precise
strike and a separate "hold it still" step with a sustained-speed, time-limited pursuit. See "Gameplay model: the

## Gameplay model: the escalation level (all **Abstraction** unless noted above)

The octopus reuses the cone-snail hunt's existing systems and tuning shape (`src/config/tuning.ts`): venom (here,
the octopus's own paralytic bite venom) with the same cost/cooldown/regen model, and a threat AI with the same
patrol → chase → grab → retreat state machine as the octopus-vs-snail threat, parameterised separately as
`TUNING.shark` (the blue shark). Cipher's own prey (the cone snails) reuses the same prey simulation
(`src/systems/fish.ts`) as the three-snail campaign's reef fish, but with its own tuning (`TUNING.snailPrey`) and
placement: scattered individuals instead of schools, since cone snails are solitary foragers (see the evidence
table row above).

Cipher's own abilities no longer reuse Catalyst's `strike`/`lock`/`reel` skeleton unchanged — only `reel` (Draw to
Mouth) still does. Camouflage is a new ability kind (`camouflage` in `src/characters/types.ts`): activating it sets
a countdown (`HuntState.camouflage`) that does two things while it runs, both read in `stepHunt`
(`src/systems/hunt.ts`) — Cipher's top speed is multiplied down (a real tradeoff: crypsis costs mobility), and the
same motion signal that both the shark's sightRadius detection (`stepPredator`) and nearby prey's awareness
(`stepAwareness`) already key off is scaled down by a `detectionMultiplier` — not to zero, so camouflage reduces
rather than eliminates detection, matching how "holding still" already works for every other character. The window
ends on its own, or instantly the moment Pounce fires (hit or miss), reusing the real tradeoff that a sudden lunge
reveals the animal. Pounce is still the `strike` ability kind, with one addition: an `instantGrip` flag that sets
the target's motor-lock to maximum the instant it hits, so there's no separate Lock step for the octopus — the
paralytic bite is modeled as happening as part of the one synchronous grab (see the evidence table above).

## Gameplay model: the orca pod (all **Abstraction** unless noted above)

Glaucus's threat reuses `src/systems/predator.ts`'s single-threat state machine (patrol → chase → grab → retreat),
run once per pod member (`HuntState.pack`, stepped alongside `HuntState.predator` in `stepHunt`), parameterised as
`TUNING.orca`. The only new logic is pack coordination: each frame, if any pod member is already chasing, every
other member still patrolling gets its own awareness nudged up by `TUNING.orcaPod.alertGain · dt`, so it tends to
start chasing shortly after, rather than only reacting to the player on its own. This models the real, cited fact
that orca pods hunt cooperatively, without claiming any specific signalling mechanism (echolocation, vocal calls, or
otherwise) — none is cited here. `TUNING.orcaPod.size` (3) is a gameplay choice within, not matching, the 1–6 range
actually observed across the four documented whale-shark events in Pancaldi 2024.

## Gameplay model: Glaucus's own mechanic — Pursuit and Burst (all **Abstraction** unless noted above)

Unlike the orca pod above (which keeps the existing threat state machine unchanged), Glaucus's own abilities no
longer reuse the `strike`/`lock`/`reel` skeleton every other escalation-tier character used before this rework —
only `reel` (Swallow Whole) still does. Pursuit is a new ability kind (`pursuit` in `src/characters/types.ts`):
activating it searches for the nearest live, untethered squid within range — no cursor aim at all, a deliberate
departure from every aimed-strike character — and, if one is found, starts a countdown (`HuntState.pursuit`) during
which Glaucus's top speed is multiplied up (see `stepPursuit` and the speed-multiplier logic in `stepHunt`,
`src/systems/hunt.ts`). If the player closes to contact range before the countdown ends, the squid is caught —
tethered exactly like a strike hit, and immediately fully restrained (no separate Lock step; see the evidence table
above for why). If the countdown runs out first, the chase is lost and the squid gets away, same as a miss costs a
cooldown penalty. Burst is a second new kind (`burst`): it needs an active pursuit (denied otherwise, the same way
Lock needed a tethered fish) and extends the countdown, representing a further push of speed within an already
active chase. Both are grounded in a single real finding — real blue shark swimming speed is not constant (Sciarrotta 1977) — rather than two independent citations; see the evidence table and Open Questions above for exactly what
that finding does and does not establish.

## Gameplay model (all **Abstraction**)

All constants live in `src/config/tuning.ts`. They are balancing parameters, not measured biology.

**Metabolic module** (`src/systems/metabolic.ts`). Glucose state g, insulin-like exposure I, recovery override O, all in [0, 1]:

```
dg/dt = −drop·I·g + recovery·(1 − g)·(1 − O)
I, O decay exponentially (τ_I, τ_O); clouds add exposure while a fish is inside them
swim performance = min + (1 − min)·g²
```

O scales only the recovery term, so it does almost nothing to a healthy fish but extends the low-glucose window
after an insulin hit. With current tuning, insulin alone settles just above the catch threshold, and only the
combination makes fish catchable. This is a game rule chosen to echo the qualitative relationship in Yeung 2024,
not a model of fish physiology.

**Escape module** (`src/systems/escape.ts`). Awareness a ∈ [0, 1] rises with snail proximity and motion, scaled by
`responsiveness` (the hook for Nightshade's escape-dampening ability) and by glucose-dependent alertness, and decays
otherwise. Fish flee with hysteresis.

**Capture.** Vulnerability = 1 − g. Engulf captures up to N fish within its radius whose vulnerability ≥ threshold.

**Other abstractions.** Venom reserve, regeneration, cooldowns, a 3-fish objective, a time limit, a sinusoidal reef current.

## Open questions for expert review

- Which hunting-strategy terms (e.g., "taser-and-tether", named venom "cabals") are appropriate for each species on screen?
- Catalyst (_C. catus_): which venom-component descriptions are safe to show in plain language? (See Himaya 2015 and Schulz 2019.)
- Nightshade (_C. tulipa_): Dutt 2019 reports that ρ-TIA may act alone. Should the Ghost Signal → Silent Tide combo be described as complementary rather than synergistic?
- Is there any evidence of a somatostatin-like override component in _C. tulipa_? (None assigned until confirmed.)
- Ziconotide facts for the ending: verify the source species and approval details against an authoritative review.
- Image sourcing: licensed photographs of each species (e.g., Wikimedia Commons, with per-file license checks).
- Is _Argonauta_ (the paper nautilus) a fair stand-in for "octopus" when citing Galván-Magaña 2013? It is a true
  octopus (order Octopoda, family Argonautidae), but a pelagic, shell-less-except-for-the-female-case species with
  a very different ecology from the benthic _O. bimaculoides_/_O. vulgaris_ the rest of this section is based on.
  Flagged in the evidence table above rather than glossed over.
- The paralytic-bite ability is based on cephalotoxin research that is best characterized in other octopus and
  cuttlefish species (see Gonçalves 2021), not confirmed specifically in _O. bimaculoides_. Treated as a
  genus/class-level finding, not a species-specific one, until checked further.
- Kalmijn 1971 demonstrated electroreception-based prey detection in one named species, the small-spotted catshark,
  not the blue shark now named and drawn as Cipher's threat. The game borrows the mechanism (ampullae of Lorenzini
  are present across sharks broadly) but does not claim Kalmijn's specific finding was shown in blue sharks, or that
  blue sharks are documented predators of octopus specifically (Galván-Magaña 2013's cephalopod-predation record for
  the blue shark is the open-ocean paper nautilus, not a benthic octopus).
- Cone snails being solitary foragers (no schooling, no social hunting groups) is stated above from general
  malacological knowledge of the genus; a dedicated primary-literature citation for this specific claim has not yet
  been added to the References table. Treat it as **Hypothesis-strength until a citation is added**, even though the
  game only relies on it for an absence (no schooling behaviour), not a positive claim.
- Treating Glaucus's squid prey as non-schooling, scattered individuals (like Cipher's cone snails) is based on the
  specific squid species named as dominant blue-shark prey in Markaida 2010 and Galván-Magaña 2013, which are mostly
  meso- to bathypelagic and described as "non-active" — not on a general claim that squid never school. Some squid
  species (e.g., market squid, _Doryteuthis opalescens_) are well known to school; this is a deliberate simplification
  for the specific prey species the citations name, not a universal statement about the genus the way the cone-snail
  claim (above) is intended to be.
- The automatic restraint on a Pursuit catch (formerly a separate Crushing Bite ability) has no cited bite-force or
  bite-mechanics source for _Prionace glauca_ specifically — it's a straightforward gameplay abstraction ("a hard
  bite stops the prey"), flagged as such in the evidence table above rather than invented a citation for it.
- Josef 2012 (Cipher's Camouflage) studied _Octopus cyanea_ and _O. vulgaris_, not _O. bimaculoides_. The claim
  carried into the game — rapid, chromatophore-driven background matching used as an antipredator defence — is
  treated as a genus-level finding until a species-specific source is added, the same way the paralytic-bite
  citation (Gonçalves 2021) already is for Pounce.
- Bidel 2022's synchronous-vs-sequential arm recruitment finding was measured for crabs (synchronous) and shrimp
  (sequential), not cone snails. Pounce's `instantGrip` (an immediate, synchronous-style grab with no separate Lock
  step) generalises the crab case to cone snails because both are hard-shelled, benthic prey — not because
  synchronous recruitment on cone snails specifically has been observed or reported anywhere found so far.
- Sciarrotta 1977 (Glaucus's Pursuit and Burst) documents a real, measured **diel** (day/night) swimming-speed
  pattern in blue sharks — speed and movement rate rise at night, specifically — not an on-demand burst a shark
  chooses to trigger. The game does not model day/night at all, so only the general, weaker claim is carried into
  the mechanic: real blue shark swimming speed varies over time rather than holding at one constant cruising speed.
  The specific speed multiplier, chase duration, and Burst's extra time are gameplay abstractions with no numeric
  source, the same way Catalyst's Motor Lock potency or Reel In's reel speed never claimed a measured source either.
- Orca pack-hunting of sharks is documented against juvenile whale sharks (Pancaldi 2024, Gulf of California) and a
  white shark via genetic/DNA evidence from a carcass (Reeves 2025, Australia) — not against the blue shark the
  player controls as Glaucus. The game states this plainly rather than implying the citations are about the blue
  shark; see "Orca pod chosen over a direct blue-shark citation" above. Earlier South African observations of orcas
  preying on white sharks (Gansbaai, Alison Towner and colleagues) were found in preliminary research but not
  independently verified against a primary source before this was written, so they are not cited here — only the
  two sources that were checked directly.
- Reeves 2025 notes that population assignment couldn't be confirmed from the genomic evidence alone, so whether the
  Australian white-shark kill was a coordinated group hunt (as in the Gulf of California whale-shark events) or an
  individual kill with packmates nearby is not fully settled by that paper alone; at least three catalogued
  individuals were sighted together that day. The game's "coordinated pod" framing leans on Pancaldi 2024, where
  cooperative hunting is explicitly described, plus Reeves 2025 as corroborating evidence that orcas prey on sharks
  this way more than once, in more than one place.

## Predators (for the octopus-vs-snail threat in the main campaign)

The abstract of Dutertre 2014 mentions cephalopods (octopus and relatives) and fish as predators that cone snails
defend themselves against. It does not mention sharks. Cone snails that hunt other snails also eat cone snails. The
game's octopus threat in the three-snail campaign, and how it spots and chases the snail, are gameplay inventions.
This is unchanged by the real octopus prey-capture research added above for the separate escalation level, which
documents how an octopus hunts fish, not how it would catch a cone snail.
